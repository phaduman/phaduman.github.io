Work/food mismatchWhy carbs are the big knob

1.1The mismatch

Our work changed. Our plate didn't.

For almost all of human history we were hunter-gatherers. Grains arrived with farming only ~10,000 years ago, and for most of the time since, they fuelled long days of hard physical work in fields and later factories. In the last ~50 years, many city jobs moved to a desk. The fuel stayed the same; the work didn't.

1.2The fat

"Stored as fat", but where it goes matters

Excess carb raises insulin, and insulin tells the body to store the surplus as fat. That fat lands in two very different places: one mostly harmless, one that behaves like an active, trouble-making organ.

Tan layer = subcutaneous (under skin)
Red = visceral (around organs)
Under the skin

Subcutaneous fat

The soft, pinchable layer on hips, thighs, arms and belly. Mostly a passive fuel store and insulation. Cosmetically annoying, but relatively low metabolic risk.

Not the priority

Quick check for visceral fat: a waist (at the navel) more than half your height is a common warning sign. A DEXA scan measures it directly.

Deep in the belly

Visceral fat: an active organ, not just storage

Packed around the liver, pancreas, intestines and heart. You can't pinch it; a growing waistline is the clue. It doesn't just sit there. It constantly releases fat and chemical signals into the blood.

Floods the liverDrains straight to the liver, pushing fat into it → fatty liver.
Blocks insulinMakes cells deaf to insulin → the body makes even more insulin → more fat stored.
Scrambles hormonesLowers protective hormones (adiponectin) and upsets appetite and stress signalling.
Leaks inflammationReleases inflammatory messengers (e.g. IL-6, TNF-α) → constant low-grade inflammation body-wide.
Chronic inflammation keeps the immune system on alert — a plausible fuel for autoimmune flare-ups. That's why Step #0 targets visceral fat first.
An active research area. Visceral fat's links to diabetes, fatty liver and heart disease are well established; its wider impact on immunity, autoimmune conditions, brain health and more is still being actively studied.
Our work changed in 50 years. Our plate didn't.
Shouldn't we adjust what we eat to how we work?

Grains fuelled thousands of years of hard physical labour; they weren't the problem then. For many desk-bound city jobs, that fuel has nowhere to go and gets stored as fat. That mismatch is why cutting carbs is Knob 1.

Next: the plan →

Dates are approximate: the genus Homo is roughly 2–3 million years old; farming began ~10,000–12,000 years ago (earlier in some regions, later in others); the industrial revolution started in Britain in the late 1700s and spread over the next century. "Last ~50 years" is a rough, global figure. In the US, for example, in 1960 nearly half of private-sector jobs needed at least moderate physical activity; by 2008 fewer than 20% did (Church et al., PLoS One, 2011). In many fast-growing economies the shift to desk work came later, often within the last 25–30 years. Hunter-gatherer diets varied widely (some groups ate plenty of tubers, honey or wild grains), so the point isn't "zero carbs"; it's matching fuel to the work you actually do. Work / carb bars are illustrative, not measured.

2.1Start here

Where are you starting from?

This plan is built around autoimmune conditions, but the same levers apply to several common metabolic problems. You may be dealing with one or more of these:

AutoimmuneVitiligo, MS, rheumatoid arthritis, celiac, Hashimoto's and more
Prediabetes / type 2 diabetesRising blood sugar, high HbA1c, insulin resistance
Heart issuesHigh blood pressure, high triglycerides, poor cholesterol profile
Gut issues: IBSBloating, cramps, irregular bowels, food sensitivities
Fatty liver & belly weightRaised liver enzymes, a growing waistline, and more
The common thread: many of these share the same roots: excess carb, insulin resistance and visceral fat (see part 1). Turning the same knobs can help more than one at once.

On diabetes or blood-pressure medication? Cutting carbs can lower blood sugar and blood pressure quickly, so doses may need adjusting. Work with your doctor before and during the change.

2.3Turn the knobs

Three big knobs to turn

Knob 1: cut carbs Knob 2: add healthy fat Knob 3: maintain protein

Lifestyle shifted from manual labour that burned off carbs to a sedentary one that stores the excess as fat (see part 1). Excess carb is now a serious health risk. It's the first big knob to turn.

Excess carb→ Insulinconverts to fat→ Visceral fattarget: reduceSubcut. fatnot a priority
Knob 1: cut carbs

Cut excess carb

Less insulin → less visceral fat around the liver, pancreas & heart. Helps the body recover from insulin resistance and improve insulin sensitivity. Metabolic markers can start shifting within weeks, but how long before you feel it varies a lot, from a few months to a year or more.

Knob 2: add healthy fat

Healthy fat → energy & absorption

Fat fuels ketone production for steady energy, and carries fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E & K into the body.

Knob 3: maintain protein

Maintain protein

Maintain healthy protein intake daily, sourced ethically and, as much as practical, organically.

Goal: shrink visceral fat while feeding the body clean fuel through healthy fat and steady protein. Together they help the liver, pancreas & heart regulate hormones; inflammation can drop, and the immune system's overreaction can calm over time. See what to eat →

Diet suspects to check
GlutenWheat, barley, rye; also hidden in soy sauce, sauces and soups. Proven trigger for celiac. Ask for a celiac test before cutting it.
DairyCould be the sugar (lactose) or the proteins (whey, casein). Lactose-free milk only removes the sugar; the proteins stay.
Seed oils: cooking / fryingIndividual food types

Other common suspects: eggs, soy, corn, nightshades (tomato, potato, peppers, eggplant), nuts, legumes, alcohol. Most people tolerate them; test only if symptoms persist.

Environmental suspects to check

Not every body reacts to these; some do. Use the same remove → reintroduce → track approach to find yours.

Air pollution & dustTraffic and smoke, construction or factory dust (e.g. silica)
Heavy metals in foodMercury in large fish, arsenic in rice, cadmium
Certain infections or medicationsE.g. Epstein-Barr virus; some prescription drugs. Talk to your doctor; don't stop medication on your own
ChemicalsSolvents, "forever chemicals" (PFAS, e.g. worn non-stick pans), hair dye, pesticides
Mold & damp buildingsCheck the washer detergent tray, bathrooms, AC units and damp walls

Prolonged exposure to many of these can cause inflammation and trigger an autoimmune response. Track changes for 4–6 weeks before drawing conclusions.

2.5Outcome

Body starts to recalibrate

Over time, hormones can stabilize, inflammation can calm, and the autoimmune trigger may fade. Mileage varies: some people notice changes within a few months, while others may not see clear benefit for up to a year or longer. Consistency matters more than speed.

This is step #0: more knobs coming
In my words: "Decoding the autoimmune can be a long journey, full of frustrations and failures... As a method of elimination of what might be causing the trigger — Step #0: reduce/eliminate visceral fat. Some lucky people can just go the keto diet and swing back to a healthy state; for others, the journey could be much longer." — Phalguni Nanda

3.1Knob 1: cut carbs

Carb: what to eat, what to avoid

This is the big knob. Turn it first. Everything below follows from one idea.

Don't count calories. Count carbs.
Not just added sugar. Every carb counts: rice, roti, potatoes, fruit, dairy sugar, all of it.
20g / day
First ~3 months: the major shift
30–50g / day
Then: maintenance

The 3 months is a starting phase, not a promise of results. Results vary widely: some see changes within months, others take up to a year or more. Track your markers (2.2) rather than the calendar.

On insulin or other diabetes medication? Talk to your doctor before cutting carbs this much. Reducing carbs without adjusting your dose can send blood sugar dangerously low.

Fasting / intermittent fasting

When you eat matters too. A simple pattern: no food from 8 PM to 12 noon, then eat your meals between noon and 8 PM. That's a 16-hour fast, often called 16:8. Most of it happens while you sleep.

Fasting · 16 h
Eating · 8 h
8 PM12 noon8 PM
Insulin stays low for longerNo food means no insulin spikes, so the body switches to burning stored fat, including visceral fat.
Better insulin sensitivityRegular low-insulin stretches help cells respond to insulin again, which is the same goal as Knob 1.
Calmer inflammationFasting periods are linked to lower inflammatory markers and to cell clean-up (autophagy).
Simpler daysFewer meals means fewer carb decisions. It also echoes how humans ate for most of history (part 1).
Ease in: start with 12 hours (8 PM–8 AM), move to 14, then 16 over a few weeks. Water, black coffee and plain tea are fine during the fast. If you fast, drop the breakfast row in the example days above and fit the rest into your eating window.

Check with your doctor first if you take insulin or other diabetes or blood-pressure medication, are pregnant or breastfeeding, are underweight, or have a history of eating disorders. Research on fasting is promising and ongoing; some of its benefit comes simply from eating less.

Individual conditions can change all of this. Always get medical advice for underlying conditions. Once the major shift is done (roughly the first 3 months at 20g/day, longer for some), moderation is the general, sensible advice from most dietitians, and one this plan agrees with.

Eating out: what works
Salad bars & cafeteriasBuild a bowl: greens + a protein (tuna, chicken, eggs, tofu) + nuts or seeds + olive-oil dressing. Go easy on corn, beans, croutons and sweet dressings.
Grill & seafood placesGrilled fish or chicken with vegetables or salad. Ask to swap fries, rice or bread for extra veg.
Indian restaurantsTandoori or grilled items, fish curry, palak paneer, bhindi, baingan bharta. Skip the rice, naan and roti; order an extra sabzi instead.
Soup & sandwich spotsClear or vegetable soups plus a salad. Skip the bread, and avoid soups thickened with potato, flour or noodles.
